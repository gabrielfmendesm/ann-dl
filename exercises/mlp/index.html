    MLP — Understanding Multi-Layer Perceptrons

    One step of backpropagation worked by hand, then a multi-layer perceptron written from scratch with NumPy and trained on three problems: two classes that no straight line separates, three classes with two clusters each, and the same three classes with a second hidden layer. No neural-network library is used: the activations, the losses, the forward pass, every gradient and the update are in code/mlp.py. scikit-learn is used only to generate and split the datasets (make_blobs, make_classification, train_test_split), for the logistic-regression baselines the statement allows, and for the confusion matrix.

    Approach. The code is split into files under code/: mlp.py (the network and its losses, written once), common.py (settings, the two datasets and plotting helpers), exercise1.py to exercise4.py (the experiments), gradient_check.py (a finite-difference check of the backward pass) and main.py, which runs everything and writes results/results.json; check_report.py then confirms that the numbers of this report match that file. Every number and figure in this report is reproduced by installing requirements.txt and running, from the repository root:

    python docs/exercises/mlp/code/main.py
python docs/exercises/mlp/code/check_report.py

    Seeds. Every network starts from rng = np.random.default_rng(42), created afresh for each training run, so that the Exercise 3 and Exercise 4 networks start from literally the same seed, as Exercise 4 requires; the datasets and the train/test splits use random_state=42. Exercise 4 repeats the comparison with the generators of seeds 42 to 51.

    Training. All three trained networks use the same recipe: tanh hidden layers, LeCun-normal initialization (weights drawn from N(0, 1/fan-in), biases zero), and full-batch gradient descent, one update per epoch over the whole training set, with learning rate 0.5 for 2000 epochs. The recipe was fixed before the comparisons were run and is never tuned per exercise.

    Challenges.

    • One network for every task. Exercises 2, 3 and 4 must reuse the same forward pass, backward pass and update. The loss is therefore an object passed to the network: it turns the output pre-activation into a prediction and supplies ∂L/∂u, the only place where the tasks differ. Even Exercise 1 is checked with the same class, with a tanh output and the squared error.
    • Trusting hand-derived gradients. gradient_check.py compares every analytic gradient with a central finite difference, for all three losses, on networks with two hidden layers. The largest relative error is 6.6 × 10⁻⁸.
    • A fair depth comparison. The deeper network changes depth and nothing else: same data, split, loss, learning rate, epochs, initialization rule and seeds, and ten seeds instead of one, because a single seed points the wrong way here.

    Exercise 1

    One step of backpropagation, by hand.

    The network has 2 inputs, one hidden layer of 2 tanh neurons and 1 tanh output, with the squared error on one sample, L = (y − ŷ)². The values of the statement are

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ -0.2 \end{bmatrix},\qquad y = 1.0,\qquad \eta = 0.3, \\ \mathbf{W}^{(1)} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 & -0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.2 \end{bmatrix}, \\ \mathbf{W}^{(2)} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.3 \end{bmatrix},\qquad b^{(2)} = 0.2 . \end{aligned} \]

    Every number below is computed by hand and shown to 6 decimal places; each step uses the unrounded value of the previous one. code/exercise1.py runs the same network through the MLP code of the other exercises and prints every one of these numbers as a check.

    A — Forward pass

    Hidden pre-activations, one row of W⁽¹⁾ per hidden neuron:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 \cdot 0.5 + (-0.1)(-0.2) + 0.1 \\ 0.2 \cdot 0.5 + 0.4 \cdot (-0.2) + (-0.2) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.15 + 0.02 + 0.1 \\ 0.10 - 0.08 - 0.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.270000 \\ -0.180000 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    Hidden activations:

    \[ \mathbf{h}^{(1)} = \tanh(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}) = \begin{bmatrix} \tanh(0.27) \\ \tanh(-0.18) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.263625 \\ -0.178081 \end{bmatrix} \]

    Output pre-activation:

    \[ \begin{aligned} u^{(2)} = \mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{h}^{(1)} + b^{(2)} &= 0.5 \cdot 0.263625 + (-0.3)(-0.178081) + 0.2 \\ &= 0.131812 + 0.053424 + 0.2 = 0.385237 \end{aligned} \]

    Output and loss:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y} &= \tanh(0.385237) = 0.367247, \\ L &= (y - \hat{y})^2 = (1 - 0.367247)^2 = 0.632753^2 = 0.400377 \end{aligned} \]

    B — Backward pass

    The chain starts at the loss and moves one link at a time towards the input. Two facts are used throughout: d tanh(a)/da = 1 − tanh²(a), and every bias enters its layer with an input of 1.

    1. Loss with respect to the output. From L = (y − ŷ)²:

    \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} = -2\,(y - \hat{y}) = -2 \cdot 0.632753 = -1.265507 \]

    2. Through the output tanh. Since ŷ = tanh(u⁽²⁾), ∂ŷ/∂u⁽²⁾ = 1 − ŷ² = 1 − 0.134870 = 0.865130, and

    \[ \delta^{(2)} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{(2)}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial u^{(2)}} = -1.265507 \cdot 0.865130 = -1.094828 \]

    3. Output layer. u⁽²⁾ = W⁽²⁾₁ h₁ + W⁽²⁾₂ h₂ + b⁽²⁾, so ∂u⁽²⁾/∂W⁽²⁾ⱼ = hⱼ and ∂u⁽²⁾/∂b⁽²⁾ = 1:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(2)}} &= \delta^{(2)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(1)\top} = -1.094828 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0.263625 & -0.178081 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.288624 & 0.194968 \end{bmatrix}, \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}} &= \delta^{(2)} = -1.094828 \end{aligned} \]

    4. Back to the hidden activations. Each hᵢ reaches u⁽²⁾ through its weight W⁽²⁾ᵢ, so ∂u⁽²⁾/∂hᵢ = W⁽²⁾ᵢ:

    \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}^{(1)}} = \delta^{(2)}\,\mathbf{W}^{(2)\top} = -1.094828 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ -0.3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.547414 \\ 0.328448 \end{bmatrix} \]

    5. Through the hidden tanh. With 1 − h₁² = 1 − 0.069498 = 0.930502 and 1 − h₂² = 1 − 0.031713 = 0.968287, element by element:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \delta^{(1)} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(1)}} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}^{(1)}} \odot \left(1 - \mathbf{h}^{(1)} \odot \mathbf{h}^{(1)}\right) \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.547414 \cdot 0.930502 \\ 0.328448 \cdot 0.968287 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.509370 \\ 0.318032 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    6. Hidden layer. zᵢ = W⁽¹⁾ᵢ₁ x₁ + W⁽¹⁾ᵢ₂ x₂ + b⁽¹⁾ᵢ, so ∂zᵢ/∂W⁽¹⁾ᵢⱼ = xⱼ and ∂zᵢ/∂b⁽¹⁾ᵢ = 1. The weight gradient is the outer product of the deltas with the input:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(1)}} &= \delta^{(1)}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.509370 \cdot 0.5 & -0.509370 \cdot (-0.2) \\ 0.318032 \cdot 0.5 & 0.318032 \cdot (-0.2) \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.254685 & 0.101874 \\ 0.159016 & -0.063606 \end{bmatrix}, \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(1)}} &= \delta^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.509370 \\ 0.318032 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    The signs say what the update will do. δ⁽²⁾ < 0 means that raising u⁽²⁾ lowers the loss, which is right: ŷ = 0.367247 is below the target 1. Through W⁽²⁾ = [0.5, −0.3], the first hidden neuron should therefore move up and the second down, which is exactly the sign pattern of δ⁽¹⁾. The hidden deltas are also smaller than the output delta, |δ⁽¹⁾| = 0.509370 and 0.318032 against |δ⁽²⁾| = 1.094828: on the way back, the output delta is multiplied by a weight (0.5 or −0.3) and by a tanh derivative (0.930502 or 0.968287).

    C — Parameter update

    Gradient descent, p ← p − η ∂L/∂p with η = 0.3, applied to every weight and bias:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{W}^{(2)} &\leftarrow \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.3 \end{bmatrix} - 0.3 \begin{bmatrix} -0.288624 & 0.194968 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 + 0.086587 & -0.3 - 0.058490 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.586587 & -0.358490 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
    \[ b^{(2)} \leftarrow 0.2 - 0.3 \cdot (-1.094828) = 0.2 + 0.328448 = 0.528448 \]
    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{W}^{(1)} &\leftarrow \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 & -0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix} - 0.3 \begin{bmatrix} -0.254685 & 0.101874 \\ 0.159016 & -0.063606 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 + 0.076405 & -0.1 - 0.030562 \\ 0.2 - 0.047705 & 0.4 + 0.019082 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.376405 & -0.130562 \\ 0.152295 & 0.419082 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{b}^{(1)} &\leftarrow \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.2 \end{bmatrix} - 0.3 \begin{bmatrix} -0.509370 \\ 0.318032 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 + 0.152811 \\ -0.2 - 0.095410 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.252811 \\ -0.295410 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
    Parameter Before Gradient After
    W⁽¹⁾₁₁ 0.3 −0.254685 0.376405

    Would the loss go up or down on a second forward pass? Down. Every parameter moved against its own gradient, so to first order the change of the loss is the sum of −η (∂L/∂p)² over all parameters: −η ‖∇L‖² = −0.3 · 1.785141 = −0.535542, negative unless every gradient is zero. The step is not small, so this first-order estimate overshoots, but the loss still falls. Repeating the forward pass with the updated parameters gives z⁽¹⁾ = [0.4671, −0.3031], h⁽¹⁾ = [0.4359, −0.2941], u⁽²⁾ = 0.8896, ŷ = 0.7112 and L = 0.0834, down from 0.4004. With y = 1, overshooting is not possible here: a tanh output always stays below 1, so the only way the step could raise the loss would be to push ŷ the wrong way, and ŷ moved from 0.3672 to 0.7112, straight towards the target. The lecture's sigmoid example shows the same: one step lowers its loss from 0.525 to 0.407.

    """Exercise 1 — one step of backpropagation, checked by the MLP code.

The derivation is done by hand in the report. This script runs the same network
through the from-scratch MLP (MeanSquaredError: tanh output, N = 1) with the
weights of the statement, and prints every intermediate value so that each
hand-computed number can be compared with it.
"""

import numpy as np

from common import SEED
from mlp import MLP, MeanSquaredError

X = np.array([[0.5, -0.2]])
Y = np.array([[1.0]])
W1 = np.array([[0.3, -0.1], [0.2, 0.4]])
B1 = np.array([0.1, -0.2])
W2 = np.array([[0.5, -0.3]])
B2 = np.array([0.2])
ETA = 0.3


def fmt(a):
    return np.array2string(np.asarray(a, dtype=float).squeeze(), precision=6, floatmode="fixed", sign="-")


def run():
    print("=" * 72 + "\nEXERCISE 1 — one step of backpropagation\n" + "=" * 72)
    net = MLP([2, 2, 1], MeanSquaredError(), np.random.default_rng(SEED))
    net.W, net.b = [W1.copy(), W2.copy()], [B1.copy(), B2.copy()]   # the statement's values replace the random start

    # A — forward pass
    U = net.forward(X)
    z1 = X @ W1.T + B1
    h1 = net.activations[1]
    y_hat = net.loss.output(U)
    loss = net.loss.value(U, Y)
    print(f"z1 = {fmt(z1)}\nh1 = {fmt(h1)}\nu2 = {fmt(U)}\ny_hat = {fmt(y_hat)}\nL = {loss:.6f}")

    # B — backward pass (the chain, link by link, then the gradients returned by the MLP)
    dL_dyhat = -2.0 * (Y - y_hat)
    dL_du = net.loss.delta(U, Y)
    dL_dh = dL_du @ W2
    dL_dz = dL_dh * (1.0 - h1 ** 2)
    grad_W, grad_b = net.backward(U, Y)
    print(f"\ndL/dy_hat = {fmt(dL_dyhat)}\ndtanh/du = 1 - y_hat^2 = {fmt(1 - y_hat ** 2)}\ndL/du2 = {fmt(dL_du)}")
    print(f"dL/dW2 = {fmt(grad_W[1])}\ndL/db2 = {fmt(grad_b[1])}")
    print(f"dL/dh1 = {fmt(dL_dh)}\n1 - h1^2 = {fmt(1 - h1 ** 2)}\ndL/dz1 = {fmt(dL_dz)}")
    print(f"dL/dW1 =\n{fmt(grad_W[0])}\ndL/db1 = {fmt(grad_b[0])}")
    assert np.allclose(grad_W[0], np.outer(dL_dz, X)) and np.allclose(grad_b[0], dL_dz)

    # C — parameter update and a second forward pass with the updated parameters
    net.step(grad_W, grad_b, ETA)
    U_new = net.forward(X)
    loss_new = net.loss.value(U_new, Y)
    print(f"\nupdated W2 = {fmt(net.W[1])}\nupdated b2 = {fmt(net.b[1])}")
    print(f"updated W1 =\n{fmt(net.W[0])}\nupdated b1 = {fmt(net.b[0])}")
    print(f"second forward pass: y_hat = {fmt(net.loss.output(U_new))}, L = {loss_new:.6f} "
          f"(was {loss:.6f}, change {loss_new - loss:+.6f})")

    return {"z1": z1.ravel(), "h1": h1.ravel(), "u2": float(U[0, 0]), "y_hat": float(y_hat[0, 0]), "loss": loss,
            "W1": net.W[0], "b1": net.b[0], "W2": net.W[1].ravel(), "b2": float(net.b[1][0]), "loss_after": loss_new}
    code/gradient_check.py
    """Check the hand-derived backward pass against central finite differences.

For every loss the MLP supports, a small random network is built and each
analytic gradient entry is compared with (L(p + e) - L(p - e)) / (2e). The
finite differences are used only as a check: training uses the analytic
gradients of mlp.py.
"""

import numpy as np

from common import SEED
from mlp import MLP, BinaryCrossEntropy, CategoricalCrossEntropy, MeanSquaredError

EPS = 1e-6


def max_relative_error(net, X, y):
    Y = net.loss.targets(y)
    grad_W, grad_b = net.backward(net.forward(X), Y)
    worst = 0.0
    for params, grads in ((net.W, grad_W), (net.b, grad_b)):
        for P, G in zip(params, grads):
            for idx in np.ndindex(P.shape):
                saved = P[idx]
                P[idx] = saved + EPS
                up = net.loss.value(net.forward(X), Y)
                P[idx] = saved - EPS
                down = net.loss.value(net.forward(X), Y)
                P[idx] = saved
                numeric = (up - down) / (2 * EPS)
                worst = max(worst, abs(numeric - G[idx]) / max(1e-8, abs(numeric) + abs(G[idx])))
    return worst


def run():
    print("\n" + "=" * 72 + "\nGRADIENT CHECK — analytic backward pass vs. central finite differences\n" + "=" * 72)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
    X = rng.normal(size=(20, 3))
    # each case: (name, network, targets); the generator is consumed in this order
    cases = [("binary cross-entropy, sizes [3, 5, 4, 1]",
              MLP([3, 5, 4, 1], BinaryCrossEntropy(), rng), rng.integers(0, 2, 20)),
             ("categorical cross-entropy, sizes [3, 5, 4, 3]",
              MLP([3, 5, 4, 3], CategoricalCrossEntropy(3), rng), rng.integers(0, 3, 20)),
             ("mean squared error, sizes [3, 5, 4, 2]",
              MLP([3, 5, 4, 2], MeanSquaredError(), rng), rng.uniform(-1, 1, (20, 2)))]
    errors = {}
    for name, net, y in cases:
        errors[name] = max_relative_error(net, X, y)
        print(f"  {name}: max relative error {errors[name]:.1e}")
    assert max(errors.values()) < 1e-6, "backward pass disagrees with finite differences"
    return max(errors.values())

    Exercise 2

    Binary classification that a straight line cannot do.

    A — Generate the data

    The data is generated exactly as given: make_blobs with centers (0, 0), (3, 3) and (−3, −3), 500/250/250 samples, cluster_std=1.2 and random_state=42. Class 1 is made of the two outer clusters, so each class has 500 points. train_test_split(..., test_size=0.2, random_state=42, stratify=y) gives 800 training and 200 test points, both exactly 50.00% Class 1.

    B — Establish the baseline

    A logistic regression (sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression, default settings) fitted to the training set scores 47.50% on the test set: worse than guessing. Item D explains why.

    C — Implement the MLP

    The network is the class MLP in code/mlp.py. It takes the layer sizes and the loss as arguments, MLP(layer_sizes, loss, rng), so the same code builds any depth and width:

    • Forward pass. Each layer computes Z = A W⁽ˡ⁾ᵀ + b⁽ˡ⁾ for a batch A with one sample per row; W⁽ˡ⁾ has one row per receiving neuron, as in the lecture. Hidden layers apply tanh, and the last layer returns its pre-activation U to the loss.

    • Loss. Here the loss is binary cross-entropy with a sigmoid output, p = σ(u), averaged over the N training samples:

      \[ L = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \Big[ y_n \log p_n + (1 - y_n) \log(1 - p_n) \Big]. \]

      The code evaluates it in the equivalent form log(1 + eᵘ) − y u, which never takes the log of 0.

    • Backward pass, derived by hand. Since dσ/du = p(1 − p), the output error is

      \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial u_n} = \frac{1}{N}\left(-\frac{y_n}{p_n} + \frac{1 - y_n}{1 - p_n}\right) p_n (1 - p_n) = \frac{p_n - y_n}{N}. \]

      From there, for every layer l going backwards, with δ⁽ˡ⁾ = ∂L/∂Z⁽ˡ⁾ (one row per sample) and A⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾ the input of layer l:

      \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}} &= \delta^{(l)\top} \mathbf{A}^{(l-1)}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(l)}} = \sum_{n} \delta^{(l)}_n, \\ \delta^{(l-1)} &= \left(\delta^{(l)} \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\right) \odot \left(1 - \mathbf{A}^{(l-1)} \odot \mathbf{A}^{(l-1)}\right). \end{aligned} \]

      These are the six steps of Exercise 1 written for a batch and any number of layers. gradient_check.py confirms them against finite differences, with a largest relative error of 6.6 × 10⁻⁸.

    • Update. p ← p − η ∂L/∂p for every weight and bias (step). fit repeats forward, backward and update once per epoch on the whole training set, recording the training loss.

    code/mlp.py
    """Multi-layer perceptron written from scratch with NumPy.

One class serves every exercise. The layer sizes and the loss are arguments;
the forward pass, the backward pass and the parameter update are the same code
whatever the task:

    Exercise 1 — MeanSquaredError (tanh output), one sample, weights given by hand
    Exercise 2 — BinaryCrossEntropy (sigmoid output), layer sizes [2, 16, 1]
    Exercise 3 — CategoricalCrossEntropy (softmax output), layer sizes [4, 16, 3]
    Exercise 4 — CategoricalCrossEntropy, layer sizes [4, 16, 16, 3]

Each loss turns the output pre-activation u into a prediction and supplies
dL/du, the error that the backward pass propagates. Hidden layers use tanh.
"""

import numpy as np


# --- Losses: each one pairs an output activation with its loss -------------------------------
# Every method receives the output pre-activation U (one row per sample). The loss is the mean
# over the N samples, so dL/dU carries the factor 1/N.

class BinaryCrossEntropy:
    """Sigmoid output, L = -mean[y log p + (1 - y) log(1 - p)], one output unit, labels in {0, 1}."""

    def targets(self, y):
        return np.asarray(y, dtype=float).reshape(-1, 1)

    def output(self, U):
        return 0.5 * (1.0 + np.tanh(0.5 * U))           # = 1 / (1 + e^-u), without overflow for large |u|

    def value(self, U, Y):
        # log(1 + e^u) - y u is the same loss written from the logit: no log(0) for saturated p
        return float(np.mean(np.logaddexp(0.0, U) - Y * U))

    def delta(self, U, Y):
        return (self.output(U) - Y) / len(Y)            # dL/du = (p - y) / N


class CategoricalCrossEntropy:
    """Softmax output, L = -mean[sum_k y_k log p_k], one output unit per class, integer labels."""

    def __init__(self, n_classes):
        self.n_classes = n_classes

    def targets(self, y):
        return np.eye(self.n_classes)[np.asarray(y)]   # one-hot rows

    def output(self, U):
        E = np.exp(U - U.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))   # shifting u by a constant leaves softmax unchanged
        return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

    def value(self, U, Y):
        # -log p_y = logsumexp(u) - u_y
        log_norm = U.max(axis=1) + np.log(np.exp(U - U.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)).sum(axis=1))
        return float(np.mean(log_norm - np.sum(Y * U, axis=1)))

    def delta(self, U, Y):
        return (self.output(U) - Y) / len(Y)            # dL/du = (p - y) / N, derived in the report


class MeanSquaredError:
    """Tanh output, L = mean[sum_k (y_k - y_hat_k)^2] (Exercise 1)."""

    def targets(self, y):
        return np.asarray(y, dtype=float).reshape(len(np.atleast_1d(y)), -1)

    def output(self, U):
        return np.tanh(U)

    def value(self, U, Y):
        return float(np.mean(np.sum((Y - self.output(U)) ** 2, axis=1)))

    def delta(self, U, Y):
        Y_hat = self.output(U)
        return 2.0 * (Y_hat - Y) * (1.0 - Y_hat ** 2) / len(Y)   # dL/dy_hat * dy_hat/du


# --- The network -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

class MLP:
    """Fully connected network: tanh hidden layers, the loss's activation on the output layer.

    layer_sizes = [n_inputs, hidden_1, ..., hidden_k, n_outputs]. W[l] has one row per
    receiving neuron (shape: out x in), as in the lecture, so a layer computes
    Z = A W^T + b for a batch A with one sample per row.
    """

    def __init__(self, layer_sizes, loss, rng):
        self.layer_sizes = list(layer_sizes)
        self.loss = loss
        # LeCun-normal initialization: standard deviation 1/sqrt(fan_in) keeps tanh units out of saturation
        self.W = [rng.normal(0.0, 1.0 / np.sqrt(n_in), size=(n_out, n_in))
                  for n_in, n_out in zip(layer_sizes[:-1], layer_sizes[1:])]
        self.b = [np.zeros(n_out) for n_out in layer_sizes[1:]]

    def n_parameters(self):
        return sum(W.size + b.size for W, b in zip(self.W, self.b))

    def forward(self, X):
        """Return the output pre-activation U; keep every layer's activation for the backward pass."""
        A = np.atleast_2d(np.asarray(X, dtype=float))
        self.activations = [A]                     # activations[l] is the input of layer l (A[0] = the data)
        last = len(self.W) - 1
        for l, (W, b) in enumerate(zip(self.W, self.b)):
            Z = A @ W.T + b
            if l == last:
                return Z
            A = np.tanh(Z)
            self.activations.append(A)

    def backward(self, U, Y):
        """Gradients of the loss with respect to every W[l] and b[l], by the chain rule."""
        D = self.loss.delta(U, Y)                  # dL/dZ of the output layer, one row per sample
        grad_W, grad_b = [None] * len(self.W), [None] * len(self.b)
        for l in reversed(range(len(self.W))):
            A_in = self.activations[l]             # input of layer l
            grad_W[l] = D.T @ A_in                 # dL/dW[l] = sum over samples of delta x input^T
            grad_b[l] = D.sum(axis=0)              # dL/db[l] = sum over samples of delta
            if l > 0:
                # back through W[l], then through tanh: d tanh(z)/dz = 1 - tanh(z)^2 = 1 - A^2
                D = (D @ self.W[l]) * (1.0 - A_in ** 2)
        return grad_W, grad_b

    def step(self, grad_W, grad_b, lr):
        """Gradient descent: p <- p - lr * dL/dp for every weight and bias."""
        for l in range(len(self.W)):
            self.W[l] -= lr * grad_W[l]
            self.b[l] -= lr * grad_b[l]

    def fit(self, X, y, epochs, lr):
        """Full-batch gradient descent: one update per epoch, using every training sample.

        Returns the training loss at epochs 0..epochs: index 0 is the initial network,
        index e the network after e updates.
        """
        Y = self.loss.targets(y)
        history = []
        for _ in range(epochs):
            U = self.forward(X)
            history.append(self.loss.value(U, Y))
            self.step(*self.backward(U, Y), lr)
        history.append(self.loss.value(self.forward(X), Y))
        return np.array(history)

    def predict_proba(self, X):
        return self.loss.output(self.forward(X))

    def predict(self, X):
        P = self.predict_proba(X)
        return (P[:, 0] >= 0.5).astype(int) if P.shape[1] == 1 else P.argmax(axis=1)

    D — Analysis

    What the MLP draws. In Figure 3 the network assigns Class 0 to a band that crosses the plane diagonally between the two outer clusters, and Class 1 to the plane on both sides of it. Along the line through the three cluster centers, from (−6, −6) to (6, 6), the prediction goes Class 1 → Class 0 → Class 1. It changes at (−1.62, −1.62) and again at (1.59, 1.59), about halfway between the middle center and each outer one. The result is 91.50% on the test set, with 94.00% of the middle cluster, 90.48% of the upper-right cluster and 87.93% of the lower-left cluster correct. The 17 remaining test errors are points that fall in the other class's region of Figure 3, where the clusters overlap (Figure 1).

    Why no straight line can produce that region. A linear classifier predicts Class 1 where w · x + b ≥ 0. On any straight path x(t) = a + t (c − a), the score w · x(t) + b = (w · a + b) + t w · (c − a) is a linear function of t, so it changes sign at most once. Every linear classifier therefore gives at most one switch along the diagonal, while the data needs two: Class 1 at both ends, Class 0 in the middle. Whatever its orientation, a line can put at most one of the two Class 1 clusters on its Class 1 side.

    Why the baseline falls below 50%, not merely below 90%. The best a line can do is give up on one outer cluster. The best line, computed exactly over every way a line can split the points, scores 75.50% on the test set (74.25% on the training set). Logistic regression does not find it, because it minimizes the log-loss, not the error count. A line that isolates one outer cluster also labels the other outer cluster as Class 0, and those 250 points lie far from the line, so they would be wrong with high confidence; the log-loss of a confident mistake grows with its distance to the line. The log-loss minimum is therefore almost flat: the fitted weights are w = [0.0296, 0.0015] and b = −0.0031 (‖w‖ = 0.0297), and every test probability lies between 0.457 and 0.544. The boundary of this near-constant model is the nearly vertical dashed line through the middle of Figure 3. It cuts the middle cluster in half (53.00% correct) and puts one outer cluster on each side, so the upper-right cluster is 100.00% correct and the lower-left one 0.00%. That adds up to about half the test set, and the sample lands at 47.50%, slightly below a coin flip. The hidden layer is what turns this impossible "both ends" pattern into a band: each tanh unit draws one soft line, and the output combines several of them into a region that no single line can describe.

def best_line_accuracy(X, y):
    """Exact accuracy of the best straight line on (X, y) for a 2D binary problem: a benchmark, not a model.

    Every line classifies by thresholding a projection u . x. Along the sorted projections the best
    threshold comes from cumulative class counts, and the sorted order only changes at the directions
    where two points project to the same value; rotating u once through all of those directions,
    swapping one adjacent pair at each, visits every way a line can split the points.
    """
    n = len(y)
    i, j = np.triu_indices(n, 1)
    d = X[i] - X[j]
    swap = np.arctan2(d[:, 1], d[:, 0]) + np.pi / 2           # u perpendicular to x_i - x_j
    angles = np.concatenate([swap, swap + np.pi]) % (2 * np.pi)
    first, second = np.concatenate([i, i]), np.concatenate([j, j])
    events = np.argsort(angles)
    angles, first, second = angles[events], first[events], second[events]

    theta = (angles[0] + angles[1]) / 2                       # start between the first two events
    order = np.argsort(X @ np.array([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)]))
    ys = y[order]
    score = (np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(ys == 0)])                    # Class 0 below the threshold
             + np.concatenate([np.cumsum((ys == 1)[::-1])[::-1], [0]]))   # Class 1 above it
    best = int(score.max())
    order, score, labels = order.tolist(), score.tolist(), y.tolist()
    pos = [0] * n
    for k, point in enumerate(order):
        pos[point] = k
    for a, b in zip(np.roll(first, -1).tolist(), np.roll(second, -1).tolist()):
        k = min(pos[a], pos[b])
        assert abs(pos[a] - pos[b]) == 1, "points swapping order must be adjacent"
        low, high = order[k], order[k + 1]
        order[k], order[k + 1] = high, low
        pos[high], pos[low] = k, k + 1
        if labels[low] != labels[high]:                       # only a swap between classes changes a count
            score[k + 1] += 2 if labels[high] == 0 else -2
            best = max(best, score[k + 1])
    return best / n

    B — Adapt the network

    The adaptation is two arguments. The network is built as MLP([4, 16, 3], CategoricalCrossEntropy(3), rng), where Exercise 2 used MLP([2, 16, 1], BinaryCrossEntropy(), rng). The output layer has 3 units, softmax turns them into probabilities, and categorical cross-entropy replaces binary cross-entropy. The forward pass, the backward pass, the update and the training loop (forward, backward, step and fit in code/mlp.py) are the same code, unchanged. Their only contact with the task is loss.delta, which supplies ∂L/∂u, and that has the same form, p − y, for both losses.

    Derivation of ∂L/∂u for softmax + cross-entropy. For one sample with output pre-activations u₁, …, u_K, softmax gives

    \[ p_k = \frac{e^{u_k}}{\sum_{m} e^{u_m}}, \qquad L = -\sum_{k} y_k \log p_k, \]

    with y one-hot, so Σₖ yₖ = 1. Differentiating log pₖ = uₖ − log Σₘ e^{uₘ} with respect to uⱼ:

    \[ \frac{\partial \log p_k}{\partial u_j} = \delta_{kj} - \frac{e^{u_j}}{\sum_m e^{u_m}} = \delta_{kj} - p_j , \]

    where δₖⱼ is 1 when k = j and 0 otherwise. Then

    \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial u_j} = -\sum_k y_k \left(\delta_{kj} - p_j\right) = -y_j + p_j \sum_k y_k = p_j - y_j . \]

    Averaged over N samples, the code uses ∂L/∂U = (P − Y)/N.

    Why it is so simple, and why that matters. The log in the cross-entropy undoes the exponential in the softmax. In isolation, each factor has a complicated derivative: the softmax Jacobian pₖ(δₖⱼ − pⱼ) and the 1/pₖ of the log. In the product, the pₖ cancels and only "prediction minus target" survives. Binary cross-entropy with a sigmoid gives the same p − y (item 2C), because it is the two-class case of the same pair. Two consequences:

    • The error signal is proportional to how wrong the prediction is, and it does not vanish when the output saturates. Pairing a softmax with the squared error would multiply the error by the softmax Jacobian, whose entries pₖ(δₖⱼ − pⱼ) all go to 0 when the output saturates, including when a confident prediction is wrong.
    • One backward pass serves every task: the output layer always starts the chain with P − Y, which is what makes the verbatim reuse above possible.

    Baseline and what the hidden layer added. A multinomial logistic regression fitted to the same training set scores 66.33%; the MLP scores 84.67%, 18.33 points more. Logistic regression gives each class one linear score and predicts the class with the highest one, so the region where a class wins is an intersection of half-spaces: a single convex cell per class, bounded by straight cuts. Here each class is two clusters on vertices two or three coordinates apart (clusters 0 and 3 for Class 0, 1 and 4 for Class 1, 2 and 5 for Class 2), and the baseline's failures sit exactly where that constraint hurts. It handles Class 0 almost as well as the MLP (80 of its 100 test samples correct, against 88), but fails on Classes 1 and 2 (57 and 62 correct, against 77 and 89). By cluster, its two worst are cluster 1 (51.02% correct) and cluster 2 (41.30%). These are exactly the only two clusters with two adjacent neighbors of other classes: cluster 1 sits next to cluster 0 (Class 0) and cluster 2 (Class 2), and cluster 2 sits next to both clusters of Class 1 (1 and 4). A convex cell for Class 1 that contains the centers of clusters 1 and 4 contains the whole segment between them, and that segment passes 1.49 from the center of cluster 2 (halfway along it, well inside cluster 2's radius of 2.45). One convex cell for Class 1 therefore either gives up part of one of its own clusters or takes in the middle of cluster 2: the straight cuts cannot follow this arrangement. The hidden layer removes the constraint: its 16 tanh units carve the space into pieces, and the output assembles each class's region from the pieces around its own clusters, a region that need not be convex.

    C — Analysis

    The result: the second layer fits the training set better and the test set no better. The deeper network reaches a lower training loss (0.1661 against 0.2595) and a higher training accuracy (93.88% against 90.62%), but not a higher test accuracy (83.93% against 84.63%). The gap between training and test accuracy grows from 5.98 to 9.95 points. The training loss and the test accuracy therefore tell different stories, and only the second answers the question.

    Why, from the structure of the data. The data is six Gaussian clusters in four dimensions, and the boundary it needs is simple: around each cluster, a few roughly linear cuts against its neighbors (Exercise 3, item D). A single layer of 16 tanh units already provides those cuts; the universal approximation theorem says one hidden layer is enough in principle, and here it is enough in practice. What the network cannot fix is that adjacent clusters overlap (ratios 0.39 to 0.45) and that about 1% of the labels are flipped: those errors are in the data, not in the model. Extra capacity cannot reduce them; it can only memorize where the training samples happen to fall inside the overlap. The deeper network has 403 parameters to the shallow one's 131, and spends them doing exactly that, which is why its training loss drops while its test accuracy does not move.

    Representation against optimization. A second layer composes features: it builds new cuts out of combinations of the first layer's cuts, which pays off when the target boundary is built from repeated or hierarchical parts. Here there is nothing to compose. The cost, on the other hand, is visible in Figure 6. With the same learning rate of 0.5, the deeper network's loss rises in some epochs: between 16 and 812 of the 2000 epochs depending on the seed, with a largest single rise of 0.1649 (the spikes around epochs 230 to 620). The shallow network's loss never rises in any epoch of any seed. The gradient now crosses two tanh layers and three weight matrices, so the same step size sits much closer to the edge of stability. The deeper network's results also vary more from seed to seed (standard deviation 2.08 points against 1.53).

    What data would reward the extra depth. Data whose structure is compositional, where a deep network reuses intermediate features and a shallow one would need far more units for the same boundary. Examples: a parity function of many inputs, a checkerboard or many-turn spiral with dozens of regions, or images, where edges combine into parts and parts into objects. Such data also needs enough samples to pay for the extra parameters, so that they learn structure rather than noise.
